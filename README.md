# Procedurality-3D

An open-universe observatory that runs entirely in the browser. You don't pilot a ship or play a character. You drift through an infinite, seeded universe and watch it: galaxies and their supermassive cores, nebulae, double and triple stars, planets, moons, climates, rivers, alien ecosystems — and the civilizations that rise, trade, war and spread between the stars. The universe keeps running whether you're watching or not. When you choose, you can also step into one creature's body and live as it for a while, or raise a **monolith** that changes a world for good. Now in 3D.

**Play:** open `index.html` in any modern browser. The whole game is one file — no build step, no server, no dependencies. Music and sound effects are streamed from the `audio/` folder next to it (keep the two together); without that folder the game still runs, just silently. Works from `file://` and on the web, on desktop and on phones/tablets — in portrait or landscape, and in true **fullscreen** (☰ menu → *Fullscreen*, the button on the title screen, or `Shift+F`).

### Galaxies, nebulae & multiple stars
The universe is no longer a uniform field of stars: every sector independently holds **one of three kinds of region** — a **galaxy** with many star systems (about 70% of sectors), a field of **scattered, independent systems** far from any galaxy (about 22%), or a completely **empty void** (about 7%; voids and fields are likelier where the cosmic web is thin).
* **Galaxies** are procedural: **spiral**, **barred spiral**, **disk** (lenticular), **elliptical**, **irregular** and **ring** galaxies, each with its own size, position in its sector, tilt and orientation, number and winding of arms, arm width, bulge, bar, ring, clumps, gas content and colours. Their stars are placed by population: young blue stars crowd the spiral arms, rings and star-forming knots, sun-like stars fill the disk, and old stars, white dwarfs, red giants, pulsars and black holes gather in the bulge and the halo — so stellar density and the mix of stars change with distance from the centre and with the type of galaxy. The unresolved light of each galaxy is drawn as a haze of glow points (with dark dust lanes along the inner edges of the arms) and resolves into individual systems as you zoom in.
* **Supermassive cores** — every galaxy has a central object chosen by its type: a quiet supermassive **black hole**, an **active nucleus** (Seyfert), a **quasar** with twin relativistic jets, a flickering **blazar**, a **radio galaxy** whose jets balloon into lobes far outside the disk, a **binary supermassive black hole** left by a merger, a dense **nuclear star cluster**, or an **intermediate-mass black hole** in small irregulars. Each has its mass, luminosity, spin and jets.
* **Cosmic structures** — emission nebulae, stellar nurseries, reflection and dark nebulae, planetary nebulae, supernova remnants, gas and dust clouds, superbubbles, globular and open clusters inside galaxies; great intergalactic clouds, cosmic filaments, intergalactic cavities, anomalous formations and **tidal streams** bridging neighbouring galaxies outside them. Their shapes are genuinely different: diffuse clouds, bubble cavities, **pillars** lit from one side, filaments and tendrils, shells and veils, **rings**, tori, **eyes**, hourglasses, helices, fans, cometary globules, dust lanes, sheets, streams, whirls, lattices, knots, crowns, jellyfish, lenses — and **composite** anomalies fused from two or three forms, some twisted, bent or flattened. Density, composition, scale, colour and morphology come from their kind, their parameters and their surroundings: gas near an active nucleus is ionised teal and violet, gas in the outskirts is bluer, structures in the arms are denser. Each one lists its shape, true size, temperature, density, mass, composition and where it lies.
* **Binary and triple stars** — about one star in eight has a companion; triples are very rare (well under one in a hundred). Companions are compatible, lighter stars of the same or other classes; **pulsars pair only with pulsars** and **black holes only with black holes**, and neither ever forms part of a triple. A binary is either **close** (the two stars circle each other in hours to months; planets can only orbit the pair from outside) or **wide** (the companion orbits tens to hundreds of AU out; planets circle the primary). A triple is **hierarchical**: a close pair orbited by a distant third star. Orbits have real separations, eccentricities and periods.
* **Companions shape the planets** — stable orbits follow the Holman–Wiegert limits: around a close pair no planet survives inside the critical distance (the whole family forms further out), inside a wide companion's reach nothing survives beyond the limit (would-be outer planets never form), and a triple allows only the band between the two. Around a close pair the two stars' combined light sets temperatures, the habitable zone, seasons and weather, and their combined mass sets orbital periods — so the types of planets and where they lie follow from the companions. In the system view the stars move on their orbits and the stability limits are drawn as dashed rings; on the globe a second sun rises beside the first around a close pair, or a distant companion shines as a brilliant star.
* **Everything is deterministic** from the universe seed and every existing star keeps its identity: the same index in the same sector is the same star (class, name and seed), a star that stays single keeps exactly the same planets and life, and scattered fields keep their stars exactly where they were. Stars that gained a companion have their planets reshaped by it, stars in galaxies are placed in their galaxy, and the stars of sectors that became voids are gone.
* On the map, click a **galaxy**, its **core** or a **nebula** to inspect it (double-click to fly to it); a galaxy's panel lists its systems, core, stellar population and structures, and a star's panel shows its galaxy, its companions and its stability zone.

### Creatures & genetics
* **27 body-plan families** — on land and in the air quadrupeds, bipeds, hexapods, octopods, serpents, gastropods, floating aerostats, the **insects** (beetles, ants, termites, bees, wasps, mantises, moths, dragonflies, stick insects and crickets, flying or flightless) and four kinds of bird (flying avians, flightless **ground birds**, **wading birds** and **swimming birds**); four orders of **amphibians** (frogs and toads, salamanders and newts, legless caecilians and fin-walking fish); in the water fish-like swimmers, whale-like **cetoids**, long-necked **marine reptiles**, **rays**, **eels**, **jellies**, **cephalopods**, **crustaceans**, **echinoderms** and chain-like **siphonophores**; and the **chimeras**, built from the parts of all the others — each built from instanced primitives and animated procedurally (walk cycles, slithering along a trail, flapping, hopping, wading, floating and paddling, swimming, undulating, jetting, pulsing, crawling over the sea floor).
* **70-gene genome** covering anatomy (size, limbs, neck, snout, jaws, beak shape, feet, tails & tail ornaments, spines, horns, antlers, ears, crests, fins, wings, eyes, armor, fur, segmentation, external gills), coloration (hues, saturation, pattern type & scale, bioluminescence), behavior (speed, aggression, sociality, perception, fertility, longevity, nocturnality, metabolism, tolerances, moist skin, diet), **breeding** (season, season length, partners per season, nest-building instinct, nest site, clutch size, parental care), **shelter** (instinct and type), **colony life** (sociality, colony architecture, caste division), **insect flight** and **insect body form**, and the seven **chimera parts**. The 25 newer genes are appended after the original 45 and drawn from separate random streams, so every species of the earlier versions keeps its genome, names, family, role and food-web links exactly (older hexapods may now live in colonies, which is the one change to how they breed).
* **Environmental adaptation** — gravity shapes limbs, climate shapes coats and ears, dim stars enlarge eyes, dense air favors fliers and aerostats, biomes drive camouflage or warning colors. Each species lists its adaptations and why.
* **Autonomous ecosystems** — agents graze, hunt, flee predators, drink, herd, sleep through the planet's real night (or day, if nocturnal), court and breed. Newborns grow up and breed in turn, so lines run through **generation after generation**: each child inherits a **crossover of both parents' genomes plus mutation** (about three quarters of its genes straight from a parent, the rest blended or mutated), and the inspector shows every individual's generation and how far its genome has drifted from the species' type.
* **Natural selection** — a climate beyond a genome's cold/heat tolerance costs energy (fur insulates), a faster metabolism moves the body faster but burns more, and armour or spines can turn a predator's strike. Better-adapted individuals outlive and outbreed the rest, so local populations adapt and diverge over the generations — or die out when conditions turn against them.
* **Real offspring** — the *Offspring* tab of a creature lists its own living young, and only them (never siblings, ancestors or strangers); each one can be tapped to inspect and follow it, and shows its other parent, generation, age and its own young. A creature without living offspring simply has no Offspring tab. The *Status* tab links to its parents. Parent–child links are kept through reproduction, generations, leaving a site and reloading the page.
* **Favourite creatures and civilization units** — tap the ♥ in a creature's panel — or in a citizen's, a warrior's, a raider's or an explorer's — to add that individual to your favourites (tap again to remove it); keep as many as you like. Creatures and civilization units are two kinds of favourite, listed apart, and each is known by its site and its id there, which never changes: a citizen is saved with its site (its city, its people, its calling, its outfit, where it was and what it was doing), so it can be found again after it moves out of view, after the simulation runs on, after you leave the site and after a reload. If its city falls (or its people vanish from the world in a new age) while you are away, you are told when you next see the site. Each one gets a heart and its name over it wherever it goes, arrows at the screen edge point to the nearest ones when they are off screen (tap one to follow it), the log reports their young and their deaths, and `L` steps through the favourites at your site. All of them are listed together in **Saves › ♥ Favourites** — portrait, species, generation, world, whether it is alive — where *Go* travels to any of them (to its world and site if needed) and follows it, and ✕ removes it. Favourites are kept on the device, not in a save slot: they survive simulation, leaving the site and reloading the page, work across universes, and travel with export/import. A favourite from an earlier version simply becomes the first of the list.
* **Fishing birds** — birds of prey whose food web includes fish hunt them the way real ones do, decided step by step by the ecosystem: they patrol low over shallow water along banks and shores, sometimes perch on the bank to watch, spot a fish when it comes up near the surface (fish rise to feed and dive when alarmed), fold their wings and plunge. Depth, the fish's alertness, light, rain, storms and wind and the hunter's own perception decide the strike; a catch is carried ashore and eaten on dry ground, a miss sends the bird back up and along the shore. Birds never settle on open water, and abandon water where nothing rises to try elsewhere later.
* **Remembered wildlife** — every site you observe keeps its creatures between visits and sessions: leaving a site (or closing the page) writes its living wildlife (ids, genomes, families, ages, needs, positions, pair bonds, clutches being carried) and the **citizens** of its cities (their city and allegiance, calling, outfit and work), its **nests** (with every egg's genome and how far along they are), its **dens and burrows**, its **insect colonies** (numbers, brood, stores, the queen's mate, and the royals inside) and who is inside which shelter, to local storage — sites saved by earlier versions restore too, their creatures taking the newer genes from their species — and landing anywhere inside it again brings back the very same individuals on the same ground (the eight most recently seen sites are kept, and always your favourites' sites).
* **Persistent wildlife** — creatures are never deleted for being off screen. Every patch of ground is populated once, the first time it is loaded; afterwards its creatures keep their exact state. Only creatures inside the rendered area are simulated — the rest are frozen in place and pick up exactly where they left off when you look again. Idle wandering stays inside the rendered area, and newcomers wander in (out of sight) only to replace creatures that died, so the view never drains or overfills. Civilizations, economies, wars and colonization are never affected by this: they run on universal time everywhere.
* **Aerostats only on gas giants** — nothing can walk, swim or land in a bottomless atmosphere, so gas giants host only floating aerostats. They ride slowly up and down between the cloud layers, drift with the jet stream, get caught in storm vortices, and when they die they sink into the depths.
* **Terrain-aware navigation** — every creature knows where it may go (walkers stay out of deep water, swimmers out of shallows, land and ice). Look-ahead probes steer smoothly around shorelines, grid A* plans routes around lakes and bays, collisions slide along boundaries, and stuck or circling creatures are detected and recover with a new reachable goal — no spinning at the water's edge.
* **Chimeras** — a family with access to the whole trait pool: every chimera is assembled from the **body**, **limbs**, **head**, **tail**, **wings**, **skin** and **appendages** of unrelated families (a serpent's body on crustacean legs with a bird's wings and horns; a beetle's head on a quadruped with a scorpion's sting; a fish's head on a whale's body walking the sea floor on crab legs), each part a gene of its own. Coherence rules keep every combination a working animal (an upright body stands on two legs, a legged body is never left without legs, a jointed body has at least four, fins only swim, a slug cannot fly), and the parts decide how it lives: broad feathered, leathery or insect wings on a light enough body **fly**, an amphibian body lives at the water's edge, the sea's chimeras swim or walk the floor. Land and sea chimeras live on most living worlds; they breed, inherit (each part whole, from one parent), mutate (a part can jump to another donor), evolve (a lineage may gain wings or trade its tail over the eras) and are named after their donors (Crabwyrm, Houndwhale, Beetlebird — or Griffin, Dragon, Wyvern, Manticore, Hippocamp when the parts line up).
* **Birds of every way of life** — besides the flying avians, worlds get up to three more bird lineages: flightless **ground birds** (moas, emus, terror birds) running on powerful legs, **wading birds** on stilt legs that stand in the shallows, strike at fish with a dagger bill or sieve the water, and **swimming birds** that float, dabble (up-ending to crop water plants), dive after fish, take wing between waters when alarmed — or give up flight altogether and fly underwater on flippers, standing upright ashore like penguins. Every bird has a **bill** (seed-cracking cone, raptor's hook, probing bill, dagger, flat dabbling bill, pouch, bent filter bill, spoonbill) and **feet** (perching, climbing, talons, running toes, wading toes, webbing, lobed toes) shaped by its family and diet.
* **Amphibians** — four orders at the water's edge on worlds with lakes, rivers, wetlands or brackish coasts: **frogs and toads** (leaping, kicking through the water, catching insects with the tongue, males calling in the breeding season), **salamanders and newts** (sprawling walkers and tail-driven swimmers, some keeping feathery external gills for life), **caecilians** (legless burrowers with ringed bodies and feeler tentacles) and **finwalkers** (fish that crutch over the mudflats on lobed fins, eyes on top of the head). They move naturally between land and water — each knows how deep it can go and which waters its skin can bear — must keep their skin wet (drying out in heat and wind, soaking it up again in water, rain or marsh), sink into **torpor** in the cold, forage for the small fry of the wetlands, and lay spawn or build foam nests and burrows; their young start life in the water as **tadpoles** or gilled larvae and **metamorphose** before they can leave it.
* **Breeding seasons, mates and nests** — breeding is written in the genome: each species breeds in its own **season** of the year (mostly spring and summer, some in autumn or winter, some all year round; the southern hemisphere half a year behind the northern, and on worlds without seasons all year), for its own length of time, and outside it no one courts. **Mating systems** are genetic too: monogamous pairs bond for life, keep together and raise their clutches together; in polygynous species a male takes several mates a season, in polyandrous ones a female does (and the males tend the eggs), promiscuous ones form no bonds. Some species build **nests**, others do not: birds weave cups and stick platforms in treetops, heap eyries on ledges up high, scrape hollows on open ground, build incubation mounds, reed nests at the water's edge and floating rafts in the shallows; quadrupeds dig dens and burrows, hexapods raise hive mounds, octopods hang egg-sac webs, gastropods leave egg jelly in the damp, amphibians lay spawn in still shallows, whip up foam nests or dig mud turrets on the flats, fish dig redds and octopuses guard dens on the bottom — each where its builder finds such a place. Nests are real objects in the world that you can tap: they take shape as they are built, hold a clutch (every egg's genome fixed at mating from both parents), are incubated or guarded by the mother, the father or both parents by turns (or left alone), can be **robbed** by hunters that can reach them (fliers and climbers in the treetops, swimmers on the sea floor), and hatch into young of the next generation that follow their parents and grow up. Clutch sizes, incubation and survival follow the species (a big clutch of spawn loses many), and a species already crowding its neighbours raises few young.
* **Dens, burrows & caves** — shelter is written in the genome and shaped by the body and the home: depending on its family, size, limbs, habitat and climate a species **digs burrows** (into soft ground or banks), **makes dens** among rocks and roots, **builds lodges** of sticks and mud at the water's edge, **takes over tree hollows** (high in a trunk for fliers), **spins silk retreats**, **digs snow dens** in the cold or **tramples lying-up beds** — or makes none. Big animals and some fliers seek out **caves**, which the terrain generates in steep hillsides and terrace risers (rock caves, **lava tubes** on volcanic ground, **ice caves** in the cold, **crystal grottoes**), each with room for so many bodies. Creatures go in to wait out **storms**, **hard frost** and the **heat of the day**, **bolt into it when chased** (hunters cannot follow), and a caring mother of a species without nests **bears her litter in her den** and nurses it there until the young can follow her; social species share one den as a group. Animals without a shelter of their own still take to a cave in a storm or when chased — or, with none they can reach, to the cover of a tree. Shelters are world objects: tap one (or a cave) to see what it is, whose it is and **who is inside now**; one left empty for long falls in.
* **Going to shelter is a journey, never a jump** — a creature picks a shelter only if there is a real way to its mouth (a clear line, or a route from the pathfinder), walks, runs, flies or swims there along that route in plain sight, slows over the mouth (a flier settles onto it rather than diving in from the sky), and then **walks in through the mouth**, shrinking into the dark of the burrow, den, cave, hollow or colony; coming out, it emerges the same way. A shelter it cannot reach is struck off for a while and another is sought — never reached some other way. A colony's worker goes in only at the colony's own entrance. Watch a favourite (or the creature you follow) and the log tells you when it senses a storm and which shelter it chose.
* **Where it sleeps is in its genes** — nightfall alone sends nobody underground. Each species has a sleeping habit (shown in its *Shelter* section), from its shelter instinct, body, habitat and social life: **shelter sleepers** (strong instinct, burrowers, snow-den diggers, big cave dwellers) go home to sleep; others sleep **in their shelter only when the night is cold, wet or stormy** and in the open otherwise; **herds and flocks bed down together out in the open**, closing in on each other before lying down, with one of them **awake on watch** by turns — the watch sees danger at full range and its alarm wakes the herd; solitary animals sleep **in the open**, under a tree when there is one close by; birds and other fliers roost. A creature that woke hungry goes to feed before it thinks of sleep again.
* **Insects & colonies** — the **insects** join the older hexapods with ten body forms and **flight** as a gene (a lineage can evolve flight or lose it). Any hexapod lineage can be **solitary** (meeting its kind only to mate, laying in burrows or clay brood cells) or **eusocial**, living in colonies modelled on four real ways of life: **termites** (a king and a queen, blind workers, big-headed soldiers, towering mounds with ventilation chimneys — cathedral spires, domes or north–south wedges — often foraging by night), **ants** (one or several queens, minor and major workers, soldiers, thatched domes or desert craters with **scent trails** radiating out), **bees** (a queen and workers, wax **combs** hanging from a bough or under a ledge; a crowded, well-stocked hive **swarms**: the old queen leaves with half the workers to found a new hive and a new queen is raised at home) and **wasps** (paper nests). Colonies are simulated: the queen lays while there are stores, the brood matures into workers, foragers go out and carry loads home (most of the colony works unseen inside), soldiers stand guard, the colony goes dormant in the cold, and when a queen dies a hive raises a new one, a termite mound a replacement and a colony with several queens carries on — otherwise it dwindles away. Every worker is a real daughter of the queen and her mate (a crossover of their genomes). In the breeding season thriving ant, termite and wasp colonies send out **winged queens and males on a nuptial flight**; each mated queen sheds her wings and **founds a colony of her own** (termite queens with a king). Hungry hunters and bold omnivores **raid colonies** for brood and stores, and the colony **alarms**: soldiers and workers pour out and **mob the intruder** — a small one is overwhelmed, a big one stung until it runs. Tap a colony to see its architecture, royals, numbers, brood, stores and the members out working.
* **Evolution over universal time** — every niche slot runs a lineage of eras. Each era the genome mutates into a new species; lineages eventually go extinct and new ones arise. This is a pure function of time, so it happens whether or not you are watching — and because it is deterministic, each species page shows its full **lineage timeline** (ancestor portraits) and forecasts its next descendant or its extinction. Population cycles follow predator–prey dynamics.

### The origin of life & microbes
Life is not a given. Every world has a **potential for life** built from five conditions, each shown with its reason in the planet's *Biosphere* tab: a **liquid solvent** (water seas, acid or methane seas, traces of water on dry worlds, droplets in a giant's cloud decks, or an **ocean under the ice** of a tidally heated moon), **temperatures** that keep it liquid, **chemistry** (carbon, nitrogen, sulfur from the atmosphere and rock), **energy** (usable starlight, or volcanic and tidal heat) and **shelter** from the radiation of harsh stars. The seed then decides whether life ever starts and when — so two similar worlds can differ, one alive and one sterile.
* **Geological time** — worlds age about 60 million years per hour of universal time, and life moves through the stages of a real biosphere: *prebiotic chemistry* → *first cells* (chemotrophs in sediments and at vents) → *microbial mats* and stromatolites once photosynthesis evolves → *complex cells* → the *first multicellular life* (algae, sponges, soft drifters) → *complex ecosystems*. The pace differs per world (slow in cold methane, capped under the ice), and the tab shows when the next stage arrives. Worlds with animals were fitted to a history that reached them.
* **Microbial evolution** — a world's microbes are **strains** with their own 12-gene genome (temperature optimum and tolerance, photosynthesis, chemosynthesis, decomposition, oxygen use, cell size and shape, motility, colonies, colour, bioluminescence). Every 20 minutes of universal time strains mutate, **compete** for the capacity of their niche (sunlit water, deep water, sediments, hydrothermal vents, hot springs, sea ice, land surfaces, cloud droplets), **split** into new lineages, **colonize** empty niches and **die out**; the great innovations (photosynthesis, complex cells, multicellularity) arrive only when the stage allows them. It is deterministic and runs while you are away; each strain has a pixel cell portrait, a Latin name that follows its shape and niche, and the recent events are listed.
* **Visible microbes** — blooms tint the sea, coloured **mats** and stromatolites carpet the shallows of microbial worlds, vents and springs take their microbes' colours, crusts cover the ground of microbe-only worlds, and luminous plankton sparkles in dark water. Plankton is the food of filter feeders and grazers at sea, so the microbial foundation feeds the whole marine food web.

### Oceans & diving
* **25 ocean zones** — each column of water is classified from its depth, temperature, light, pressure, currents (upwellings), nutrients, the shape of the floor and the nearness of land and rivers: **coral reefs** and **lagoons**, **stromatolite** shallows, **kelp forests**, **seagrass meadows**, **mangrove coasts**, **salt marshes**, **estuaries**, **upwellings**, **continental shelves** and **slopes**, the **open ocean**, **seamounts**, **abyssal plains**, **hadal trenches**, **hydrothermal vent fields**, **polar seas**, waters **under the ice**, **lakes**, **salt lakes**, **glacial lakes**, **rivers**, and **acid**, **hydrocarbon** and **lava** seas elsewhere. The sea floor has its own relief (spreading ridges with vents, seamounts, trenches), its own decoration per zone (corals, brain and table corals, fans, sponges, anemones, sea stars, kelp, seagrass, reeds, mangroves, stromatolites, crinoids, glass sponges, tube worms, manganese nodules, sea cucumbers, brinicles) and its own places — black-smoker vent fields, whale falls, wrecks off settled coasts, drowned ruins, brine pools. The planet's *Environment* tab lists its oceans by zone.
* **Marine biodiversity** — worlds with animal life get up to eight extra **marine lineages**, each bound to a home zone, the zones it ranges through and a **depth band**, and evolving through eras like every lineage. Their genomes answer the pressures of their waters: huge light-gathering eyes or lost eyes and pressure senses in the deep, bioluminescent lures and signals, red or black skin where red light never reaches, slow metabolism and deep-sea gigantism, pressure-proof proteins, heat-hardened enzymes and chemosynthetic symbionts at vents, vivid colours on reefs, weed camouflage in kelp, counter-shading and schooling in open water, blubber or antifreeze in polar seas, gigantic filter feeders in rich water, breath-hold diving for air-breathers. Roles follow (filter feeders, planktivores, grazers, detritivores, scavengers, ambush predators, stinging predators, apex predators), and the food web only links hunters and prey that share their depths and waters.
* **Marine behaviour** — swimmers keep to their zones and depths, bottom-dwellers walk the floor and follow its slope, drifters bob with the currents, **whales and marine reptiles** surface to breathe, **blow** and sometimes **breach**, **squid ink** when chased, dead bodies sink to the floor, temperature at depth stresses the ill-adapted, and food depends on the plankton of the zone and the depth. Whales need water deeper than their own bodies, so they never strand in the shallows.
* **Diving** — over water press *Dive* (`Z` or `Page Down`; `X` / `Page Up` to rise, `Shift + wheel`, or the Dive / Rise / Surface buttons, which repeat while held). The camera eases smoothly below the surface and down to the sea floor, and a depth gauge shows depth, the ocean layer (sunlit, twilight, midnight, abyssal, hadal), the zone and floor depth, **pressure**, **water temperature** and **light**. Light fades and turns blue-green with depth (red first), sunlight ripples as caustics in the shallows, visibility closes in, and below the sunlit zone — or at night — a diver's **lamp** lights the way. Marine snow drifts down, plankton fills sunlit water, bubbles rise, black smokers plume over the vents and bioluminescent sparks glow in the dark. Above the surface only a bright glare remains.
* **Integration** — sea creatures belong to the same ecosystem, evolution, saves and favourites as every other creature; weather dims the light below; microbes are the plankton; and fishers of coastal settlements put out in **fishing boats** (canoes, sailing boats or trawlers by age) that circle their fishing grounds and take real fish from the local populations.

### Climate & weather
Every world has a **long-term climate** derived from its physics, and **short-term weather** that plays out over it — nothing is hand-made per planet.
* **Climate** comes from the host star (luminosity, class and activity), the energy received at the orbit, the length of the year and of the *solar* day (a world whose year is shorter than its rotation sees the sun race across the sky), axial tilt and the seasons it drives (delayed and softened by seas and dense air), tidal locking, the tides raised by each moon and the star, atmospheric pressure and composition, the liquid that evaporates and falls (water, methane, acid — or none), ocean and land cover, biomes, elevation, volcanic regions and tidal heating. The *Climate* tab of every planet explains the result: starlight, greenhouse warming, seasons, day–night swing, tides, volcanism, circulation, moisture and which phenomena the climate allows, and why.
* **Weather** is a pure function of the world, the place and universal time, so every observer sees the same sky and the **forecast** is exact. Air masses, pressure systems, fronts and moisture drift with the planet's own circulation — trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies on fast rotators, one sluggish cell on slow ones, a fixed day-to-night flow on locked worlds — so conditions build and clear over time (clear → clouds → rain → thunderstorm → clearing) and different regions of a world have different weather at the same moment.
* **Events and their prerequisites** — rain and drizzle need a volatile and rising air; heavy rain and **thunderstorms** need warm, moist, unstable air (afternoon heating, fronts); **snow**, sleet and blizzards need sub-freezing air; **hail** needs strong storms in a mild layer; **fog** needs saturated, calm air (nights, cold water, valleys); **gales** and windstorms need steep pressure gradients; **tornadoes** need supercells with shear over open land; **tropical cyclones** (hurricanes, typhoons, cyclones with names and categories) form over seas warmer than ~26 °C on spinning worlds, track west then recurve poleward and weaken over land; **dust storms** and **dust devils** need loose dry ground and wind (or hot, calm sunshine); **volcanic eruptions** heat their surroundings and spread **ash** downwind with lightning in the plume; **auroras** glow at high latitudes of worlds with air, a magnetic field and an active star. Methane rain and snow, acid rain, eternal storms under the star of a locked world and airless worlds without any weather follow from the same rules.
* **Effects on the world** — the weather changes local temperature (and the climate's seasons and hours), visibility (fog, rain, snow, dust and ash close in the view), the sky and light, the ground (snow settling on flat ground and canopies, wet dark soil, grey ash), the water (tides raise and lower the sea along the shore, storm surge, whitecaps and rain on the surface), vegetation (autumn colour, winter browning, spring green, drought) and life: creatures shelter under trees or on shore in severe weather, flee tornadoes (which can kill), are startled by nearby lightning, see less far in fog, drink more in heat, graze less in snow and drought, fliers are pushed by the wind and fishing birds struggle in rain and storms. The soundscape follows it too.
* On the surface the *Weather* tab of the location panel shows conditions now, what the weather has left on the ground and the forecast for the next hour; the planet's *Climate* tab also shows the weather across the whole world right now, with the cyclones currently active (tap one to pin its position).
* **Performance** — weather is evaluated only where it is observed: a single place a few times a second on the surface (smoothed between samples), a coarse whole-world map refreshed in time-sliced passes on the globe, and a handful of samples for the forecast. Tornado and dust-devil cells are deterministic local vortices placed only around the observer. No fluid simulation, no per-particle physics.

### Civilizations
On some living worlds a species becomes **sapient** and founds several independent **tribes**. Each people carries a hidden progression value (XP) that grows with population, discoveries, successful activities, conflicts, trade, exploration and technology. It is never shown — you only see its effects as a civilization advances through four stages:

1. **Tribal** — tribes coexist, barter, form alliances, stay neutral or fight; wars end in peace, conquest or extermination, and allied tribes may unite.
2. **Nation** — tribes become kingdoms, republics, theocracies or leagues with diplomacy, warfare, alliances, neutrality and **international trade**.
3. **System colonization** — spacefaring peoples settle the planets and moons of their home system (never gas giants): **domed cities** on uninhabitable worlds, **open cities** on habitable ones, with supply and trade routes between colonies.
4. **Intergalactic** — they **terraform** domed colonies into new habitable worlds, travel to nearby star systems, open **interstellar trade routes**, form alliances, wage wars, conquer holdings and even exterminate rivals — including less advanced peoples they discover.

**Culture is procedural and specific to each people**: values (warlike, mercantile, devout, open…), a color-harmony palette, architecture (domed, gabled, stepped, spired, organic, monolithic, crystalline, pavilion), city layout (grid, radial, organic, linear, ringed, terraced), building material, clothing and headgear, melee and ranged weapons that evolve with each stage, vehicles, spacecraft hulls, technology aesthetic, a 7×7 heraldic emblem and a culture-specific phonology for every name.

Beyond the stage machine every people **researches** named technologies step by step, builds **great works** (libraries, cathedrals, aqueducts, observatories, space elevators, stellar gates…) that take hours to finish, **founds settlements** that can later be captured or razed, **explores** its world (mountain ranges, seas, poles, circumnavigation), launches **probes** to the other worlds of its system, sends **colony ships** and interstellar **colony fleets** that take real time to arrive, **trades named goods** (grain and furs, spices and silk, fuel cells and alloys, antimatter…), passes population milestones, and fights **named battles** at real places.

Where to see it — everything below happens in real time, on the universal clock:
* **Surface** — land on a settlement to watch it live:
  * **Construction** — cities grow house by house after a settlement is founded or a new age begins; building sites have foundations, rising walls, scaffolding, stacked materials and swinging cranes (assembly drones for starfaring peoples), and existing houses are periodically rebuilt. Finished buildings join the city; great works rise on reserved plots around the plaza.
  * **Farming & production** — crops sprout, ripen and are harvested row by row; farmers tend and harvest the fields and carry sacks to the granary, whose stock rises and falls with the harvest. Chimneys smoke, windmills turn, spaceport vents steam.
  * **Trade & transport** — market stalls with merchants, carts carrying cargo through the streets, caravans on roads between cities (foreign traders in their own colours), shuttles loading crates at landing pads, ships overhead.
  * **Fishing** — coastal settlements send out fishing boats (canoes for tribes, sailing boats for nations, trawlers later) that work their fishing grounds offshore and haul in real fish from the sea around them; wrecks lie on the sea floor off settled coasts.
  * **Research** — every city has a house of learning (shrine, observatory, laboratory or research spire) with moving instruments and scholars; a pillar of light rises from it when its people make a discovery.
  * **Exploration** — expeditions set out from the city into the wilds and plant their people's flag; flags of past expeditions stay where they were planted.
  * **War** — battles recorded by the history engine at a settlement are fought there when you watch: an army musters on dry ground and marches in, the city's warriors and militia defend it, townsfolk flee, archers and gunners keep their distance and shoot visible projectiles (arrows, bolts, rail slugs, energy bolts), buildings burn. Spacefaring enemies send fighters that duel over the city, trade bolts and are shot down. Neighbours at war also skirmish.
  * **Colonies & terraforming** — colony ships and probes lift off from spaceports (and land at colonies); terraforming processors around colonies breathe out new atmosphere while the sky slowly changes colour.
  * **Growth** — when the civilization changes (a new settlement nearby, a new age, a conquest, a ruin) the cities around you are re-planned and rebuilt on the spot, and the log tells you what happened.
* **Planet** — the *Civilization* tab, city lights sprawling across the night side, trade and supply routes between settlements with cargo moving along them, battles flaring at contested cities (and in orbit), colony ships and probes rising from their spaceports, a thickening veil over a world being terraformed, ships and orbital stations.
* **System** — freighters and supply ships flying the routes between worlds, warships massing on routes of war, colony ships and probes on their way (with wakes), colony fleets leaving for or arriving from other stars, weapons fire around contested worlds, and terraforming progress on each world.
* **Galaxy** — territories, supply/trade/alliance/war routes with ships moving along them, colony fleets crossing interstellar space, and battles flaring at the stars where they are fought.
* **Civilization dossier** — emblem, a portrait of a citizen, stage timeline, population, culture, what the people is doing *now* (current research and great work with progress, ships under way, trade partners and goods), diplomacy, colonies and holdings, and its complete **History**.
* **City panel** — an *Activity* tab with founding date, buildings standing and under construction, the next completion, the state of the fields, what its scholars study, who is in the streets, its great works, trade roads and goods, and the battles fought there.

History is simulated per **galactic region** (4×4 sectors) in 10-minute steps from the start of the region's current **age** (20–48 hours of universal time). Ages are counted from **Cycle 0**: when a universe is born — or its clock is reset — every people of the first age begins at the **Tribal** stage, with no progress at all, and advances only as its hidden XP accumulates (later ages see new peoples emerge one by one, also as tribes). It is a pure function of the universe seed and the clock, so it advances while you are away and every observer sees the same chronicle.

**Persistent history** — every civilization keeps its complete record for the whole age: discoveries and research breakthroughs, wars, battles, alliances, treaties, settlements founded, captured or razed, colonies and expansion, great works begun and completed, trade routes and trade milestones, exploration, terraforming, population milestones, new ages, disasters. Open a dossier and read it in the *History* section, filtered by category (discoveries, wars & battles, diplomacy, settlements & colonies, great works, trade, exploration, terraforming, growth & ages) and paged back to the very first entry. Because history is deterministic it is persistent without being stored: it is exactly the same after a reload, on another device or for another observer. Under heavy time acceleration the live log shows the latest events and counts the rest.

### Sound
* **Music** — *space themes* play in the galaxy and system views, *planet themes* while you orbit a world or explore its surface. Tracks don't loop back to back: after each piece there is a quiet break (short, normal or long — your choice), the same track never plays twice in a row, and moving between space and a planet fades the current piece out before the matching theme begins.
* **Rain ambience** — a seamless cross-faded loop that follows the local rain intensity on the surface, a little softer when zoomed out, and fades away when the rain stops or time is frozen.
* **Procedural nature** — (a hive or a wasps' nest hums nearby, busier by day and quiet in the cold) the rest of the soundscape is synthesized live with Web Audio from noise and oscillators, no recordings: wind with gusts (and a whistle when it blows hard), rain on leaves and ground, babbling **rivers and streams** (heard by distance and panned to where they are), **surf** rolling onto shores, **geysers** hissing and thumping as they erupt, bubbling **lava** and bogs, **thunder** after every lightning strike (later, lower and softer the farther away it struck), the howl and roar of **hurricanes**, the freight-train roar and whine of a nearby **tornado**, the whoosh of **dust devils**, **hail** pelting the ground, the hush of snow and fog (which deaden the high end and silence distant voices), the deep roar inside a gas giant, and each biome's voices — birdsong in woods by day, crickets and frogs at night, creaking sea ice, crystal fields ringing in the wind. The mix follows the terrain within earshot (sampled a few times a second), the weather and its intensity, the hour, the zoom and the air itself: thin air carries little, vacuum nothing, and frozen time is silent. Layers are created on demand and torn down when silent, one-shots are capped (fewer on phones), and it all runs from `file://` too. Its level is the *Ambience* slider.
* **Under water** — diving mutes the air's sounds to a muffled hint; below the surface you hear the waves from beneath, a deep hum that darkens with depth, bubbles, the rumble of black smokers, and the long gliding **songs of whales** and marine reptiles (lower for bigger singers). At the surface a whale's **blow** is heard when it comes up to breathe.
* **Creature calls** — each body plan has its own voice: quadrupeds by niche (herbivore, megaherbivore, forager, predator, apex predator), bipeds, hexapods, octopods, serpents, avians (ground birds boom, waders squawk, swimming birds quack and honk), gastropods and aerostats, insects, amphibians croak (a voice synthesized on the spot; frogs call for mates in the breeding season, loudest after dark), and a chimera calls with the voice of the family its head came from (a species always keeps the same variant; fish and invertebrates are silent). Creatures call when they **attack** (charging prey, striking, fighting), when they **try to escape** a predator (a higher-pitched alarm), and when you **click** them. Bigger bodies sound deeper, juveniles higher, and every individual has its own pitch. Calls are panned by screen position, fade with distance and zoom, and are rate-limited so herds and fast time stay pleasant. Sapient citizens use their species' voice.
* Interface clicks, a master/music/creature/ambience mixer, music frequency and mute live in **Settings**; `M` toggles sound. Every file was loudness-measured so all sounds sit at balanced levels, with leading silence trimmed.
* Over `http(s)` (e.g. GitHub Pages) audio runs through Web Audio (stereo panning, gain, a limiter, iOS-safe unlocking on the first tap); from `file://` it falls back to plain media elements.

### The autonomous clock
Every universe has its **own universal cycle counter**, which starts at exactly **Cycle 0** when that universe is first generated. It is derived from the real-world clock plus a persisted per-universe offset, so switching universes never carries time over from another universe or save. Orbits, planetary rotation (day/night), weather, population cycles, evolution and history are all functions of that clock — close the tab, come back tomorrow, and planets have moved and saved worlds have evolved. You can speed time up (×10, ×100, ×1000) or freeze observation (freezing is exact: no drift). *Settings → Reset universe clock* sets the current universe back to Cycle 0 and rebuilds everything from that moment; other universes keep their own time. Saves from earlier versions keep their timeline until you reset it.

**Time acceleration without desync** — creatures, citizens, projectiles and city activity are simulated in **fixed time steps locked to the universal clock** (an accumulator: 1/30 s steps at ×1, 1/15 s at ×10, ¼ s at ×100, 1 s at ×1000), with render interpolation between steps. Every step is the same size whatever the frame rate, so frame slicing never changes the outcome, and movement, behavior, reproduction, farming, construction, exploration and combat all advance in proportion to universal time at every speed. Swept movement and swept contact tests keep large steps from skipping over water or through prey. Only if a device cannot keep up does the simulation temporarily take coarser steps until it catches up. Vehicles, ships, crops, construction and storms are pure functions of time and cannot fall behind.

## Your hand in the universe

### Inhabiting a creature
Choose **Possess** in the panel of any creature — or of a citizen, a warrior, a raider or an explorer — to take over its body and live as it, in real time.
* **The controls follow the body.** Walkers walk and run (and never wade deeper than their legs allow), serpents and caecilians slither, flightless birds sprint; swimmers stay in their own waters and choose their depth (bottom dwellers crawl the floor, jellies pulse); fliers climb, glide, descend and land (never on open water), aerostats rise, sink and drift with the wind; amphibians swim and walk ashore, swimming birds dive and take wing; a colony's winged queen or male can fly. Speed, turning and what it can reach come from its own genome and anatomy.
* **Its own abilities, on buttons and keys**: the strike its body is built for (a bite, a gore with its horns, talons, a spear-bill, a sting, a pinch, a tongue-snap, a tail swipe — or, for an armed citizen, its bow, sling, javelin or gun), feeding, drinking, its call (its own kind answer and come closer), leaping, squirting ink, blowing and breaching, flashing its bioluminescence, displays of crest, spines, horns or wings that drive smaller creatures off, and rest. A strike lands on what is in front of it and within reach; armour and spines can turn it; the struck either die, bolt or turn on you.
* **Its mind and metabolism are frozen** while you are inside it: it does not age, hunger, thirst, tire or breed, and its needs stay exactly where they were. **Time acceleration is off** meanwhile (and time cannot be frozen). Everything else — other creatures, civilizations, weather — goes on normally, and can still hunt or kill you. **Esc** (or *Leave*) returns you to observing, and its own mind picks up at once from where you left it.
* Keys: WASD / arrows to move (relative to the screen), Shift to run (or fly / swim fast), Space to climb, rise or take wing, C to descend, land or dive, J its strike, K feed, V call, R rest, 1–9 its abilities, Q / E to turn the view. On touch screens: a stick and buttons.

### Monoliths
Monoliths are **never found in nature** any more: only you can raise them, on the dry ground of any rocky world or moon (**N**, ☰ › *Monoliths*, or *Monolith* in the site panel), then tap where it will stand. Each is kept with your favourites (per universe, in export/import), and each effect is a pure function of the moment it was raised, so the history engine, the lineages of life and the weather all replay the same result whenever they are rebuilt.
* **Knowledge** — the peoples of the world develop **ten times as fast**: population growth, research, great works, exploration, new settlements, colony ships, interstellar expansion, terraforming and the ages themselves (a people yet to arise there arises sooner too). Their histories record the day they found it.
* **Protection** — no civilization from elsewhere can settle the world: colony ships bound for it turn back, colony fleets pass it by, and its colonies and outposts cannot be seized. The peoples already living there — and their colonies — are untouched.
* **Chaos** — mutation runs wild: young are born with frequent, extreme mutations (giants and dwarfs, extra eyes, luminous skin, horns, spines, crests, stilt legs, new colours…), some too crippled to live long; creatures near it are twisted by its flares; strange glowing growths spread around it; newcomers from the wild are often mutants too. The changes are real genes: a giant mutant prey can outgrow its hunters, a mutant hunter take bigger prey. Mutants are listed in its panel and remembered with the site.
* **Life** — only on a lifeless world or moon: its air, warmth and water are brought into the range of life (seas fill its lowest basins, never over the monolith), and **complex life arises at once**, with its own lineages, food webs, ecosystems and biomes, from the ground up.
* **Death** — calls down a **meteor** on its own spot. You see it fall; the blast kills everything nearby and carves a **vast crater** (bowl, central peak, rim and ejecta apron, its floor glowing with melt at first) into the terrain and into the globe seen from orbit. The **extinction is planet-wide**: every lineage's chance of surviving comes from its traits (small bodies, burrows and caves, the deep sea, insects, cold-hardy slow metabolisms survive best; big hunters worst), the rest die over the following minutes of the **impact winter** (ash in the sky, a deep chill that fades over hours to a lasting one), nests, dens and colonies go with them, and the peoples of the world perish with its life (a spacefaring people lives on on its other worlds). Hours later, **new lineages radiate into the emptied niches**. Creatures of a site you were not watching die of it too, and you are told when you come back.

## Seeds & sharing

Everything is regenerated exactly from seeds — nothing is stored except your bookmarks, catalogue and settings, the wildlife and citizens of the sites you have observed, your favourites, and the monoliths you have raised.

* `ORIGIN:12,-4:3` — universe `ORIGIN`, sector (12, −4), star #3
* `ORIGIN:12,-4:3:2` — …planet #2
* `ORIGIN:12,-4:3:2:0` — …its first moon
* `ORIGIN:12,-4:3:2@14.210,-33.050` — a surface site at latitude/longitude
* `SEED~emerald dawn` — any words create a *pocket system* outside the galaxy map
* `U:ORIGIN@1200,-800` — a position on the map (galaxies and nebulae share their position this way)
* `ORIGIN:12,-4:3:2$629.1` — a civilization: its homeworld code, the age, and the tribe index

Codes appear in the URL hash, so links are shareable. Change the **universe seed** on the title screen or in *Seeds & codes* to get an entirely different infinite universe.

## Tools
* **Survey scanner** — searches outward from the current sector for worlds matching type, star class, life, species count, moons, rings and civilizations (by stage, colonies or fallen peoples).
* **Random world** — warps to a random world (usually one with life).
* **Saved** — bookmarked stars/planets/surface sites, your **♥ Favourites** (creatures and civilization units, listed apart, with *Go* and remove buttons), saved civilizations, and an automatic catalogue of every species you have observed, with portraits. Export/import as JSON (favourites and monoliths included).
* **Favourites** — the ♥ button in the menu opens the Favourites list; `L` follows the favourites at your site one after another.
* **Monoliths** — `N` or ☰ › *Monoliths*: raise one of the five kinds on the world you stand on; tap one to see what it is doing, and take down a Knowledge, Protection or Chaos monolith to end its effect.
* **Fullscreen** — true browser fullscreen; phones held sideways stay in landscape while it lasts.
* **Procedural generation details** — for any planet: the seed-hash chain, orbital/climate derivation, terrain noise parameters, the biosphere's potential, origin and stage, equirectangular elevation / temperature / moisture / biome maps, biome distribution and niche slots (land, marine, and the newer birds, amphibians and chimeras).
* **Autopilot** — the observer drifts on its own: slow orbits, and on surfaces the camera follows a random creature, switching every ~25 s.
* Settings: **graphics** — *Retro* (pixel art in the retro palette), *Neutral* (pixel art in full colour) or *HD* (smooth, high-resolution rendering; see below) — pixel size (auto or 1–6×, pixel art only), quality, outlines, **retro color quantization** (part of the graphics choice: on means Retro; always off in HD) (limits the picture to an ordered-dithered palette of 343 colours, spaced for the eye so darks stay dark; off shows full colour), vignette, FPS.

### Rendering style
The scene is drawn into a low-resolution multi-render-target framebuffer (color, view normals + outline strength, depth), then upscaled by an integer factor with nearest sampling. The post pass detects silhouettes (depth discontinuities) and creases (normal changes) per low-res pixel and darkens them, giving 1-pixel sprite outlines. Lighting is quantized into bands with ordered (Bayer) dithering; sky, nebulae, clouds and rings use dithered alpha. On the map, galaxies and nebulae are additive dithered glow points and dust is drawn with a darkening blend, so it dims the light behind it; skies show the band of the home galaxy (or a distant one) and a companion sun. The optional retro palette quantizes the final image per channel in a perceptual space with a 4×4 ordered dither. The surface uses an orthographic isometric camera.

**HD mode** (Settings › Graphics) keeps the same look — the toon light bands, outlines and palette of the world — but renders it smoothly: the scene target is sized to the display (up to 2× the CSS resolution on desktops, 1.5× on phones and tablets, within a pixel budget) and upscaled bilinearly with FXAA; the light bands blend smoothly instead of being dithered; creature shadows get a soft penumbra, cloud shadows soft edges; glows, skies, nebulae, planets and gas decks drop their stepped quantization; creatures are drawn from finer instanced primitives and, on desktops, flora, caves and buildings are rebuilt from finer ones; portraits render at twice the size. The retro palette is always off in HD. To stay fluid, the resolution follows the frame time: it steps down when frames run slow and back up when there is room. Underwater, the lit shaders attenuate light per colour channel with depth, add animated caustics in the shallows and a diver's lamp, and blend into a depth-tinted water fog; geometry above the surface fades into its glare.

### Performance
Terrain chunks and globe meshes are generated on the CPU with time-sliced budgets; planet-scale fields are sampled on a coarse grid and interpolated; vegetation is baked into one mesh per chunk and city structures into a second one that is re-baked (a couple of chunks per frame) as buildings are completed; creatures are drawn with four instanced draw calls; off-screen chunks are culled and off-screen creatures are frozen rather than simulated. The creature simulation uses a spatial hash, a per-step path-planning budget and a CPU budget proportional to the frame time. Civilization regions are built incrementally (time-sliced on the galaxy map) and advanced step by step as the clock moves; the galaxy map rebuilds its neighbourhood into staging buffers a few milliseconds per frame (nearest sectors first) while the previous one stays on screen, galaxy light and nebula particles are cached per level of detail and dimmed where crowded, and only the systems near the camera are resolved; roads are painted through a spatial index. Shelters, caves and colonies cost little: creatures inside a shelter are simulated but not drawn or collided and take no room among the creatures in the open; a colony is simulated as numbers (population, brood, stores) with only a handful of its members out in the world at a time. The *Low* quality preset (default on phones) reduces globe resolution, chunk budget, creature density and caps and citizen counts. Sea-floor life is baked into the same per-chunk vegetation mesh from the cheapest primitives; the underwater look costs a few shader instructions and a few hundred particles; microbial evolution is cached and advanced incrementally (one step per 20 minutes of universal time), and a world's ocean statistics are sampled once.
